## The value proposition

Write one sentence before any headline, for yourself, not for the page:

> [Product] helps [audience from the brief] [do the job from the brief], [the
> fact from the brief that makes it different].

Every word in it must come from the brief. If you cannot fill a slot, that is
a question for the user, not a slot to fill with an adjective. The headline,
the subhead and the section names are cut from this sentence, which keeps
them saying the same thing.

## Headline and subhead

The headline says what it is and for whom or for what outcome, in words a
stranger can repeat to someone else. The subhead says how, or for whom, or
gives the proof, ideally with a number from the brief.

Plain wins here for a measured reason: in NN/G's web-writing study, users
doubted promotional claims and the doubt got in the way of the meaning;
objective wording made the site more usable. CXL reports a test in which the
plainest headline ("Get a truck driving job with better pay") beat the
runner-up; that figure comes from a summary of a page that could not be
read, so treat it as an anecdote that agrees with the rest.

Patterns from real pages (fetched 2026-10-04; live pages change):

- Stripe: "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue." with a subhead
  that names what you can do and a scope from the first transaction to the
  billionth. Category noun, outcome, concrete range.
- Basecamp: "Basecamp is project management without all the nonsense."
  Category plus an opinionated position against competitors.
- Fintual (Chile): "Invertir es así de simple, tus inversiones reguladas" with
  a subhead that carries a number of investors and lists what you can invest
  in. Uses tú; the subhead does the proving.
- Notion: "Where teams and agents Think together." The headline names no
  category; the subhead ("Capture context, find answers, and automate tasks
  with AI built for your team") does all the work. A known brand can afford
  this. An unknown product cannot, because a visitor who reads only the
  headline leaves without knowing what it is.

Checks for a headline:

- A stranger who reads only the headline can say what the product is.
- The product name alone is a headline only when the name is already known
  (Apple can use "AirPods Pro 3"). A first product needs the category and the
  reason to care in the headline or right under it.
- A headline that only names the problem ("Stop doing X") leaves the reader
  guessing what is offered and who it is for. If you use one, the subhead
  must name the product, the audience and the outcome.
- The subhead adds something. A subhead that repeats the headline in longer
  words wastes the second most-read line on the page.
- Well-made pages keep the headline short: two to twelve words on the pages
  studied, the longest being agency statements.
  For a product or service page (judgement; the same limit applies in
  Spanish):
  - H1 and section headings: about eight words at most, one sentence, no
    colon list inside a heading.
  - When the ceiling forces a cut, cut the category words first and keep
    the brief's most specific fact in the heading. The displaced words go to
    the subhead or the next line, not to a separate generic section.

## Choosing the headline's form

The brief's strongest fact decides the form. If every headline you write has
the same shape, you are filling a template.

- Descriptive (what it is, for whom): when the category is new or the name
  says nothing, and the brief's facts are mostly about what the product is.
- Outcome or action (what the reader gets done): when the brief names a
  concrete action with a number or a visible result ("in one tap", "on one
  screen", "at your door").
- Position (against the usual way): when the brief states a stance or a
  difference from the alternatives, as Basecamp does. Only with a stance the
  user gave you; an invented rivalry is an invented claim.
- Name-led: when the name is already known to this audience.

## Benefit and feature

No test isolates benefit-led copy from feature-led copy, so this is a
working rule: lead with what changes for the reader and use the feature as
the evidence. The feature makes the benefit believable; the benefit makes the
feature matter.

| Feature only | Benefit with the feature as evidence |
|---|---|
| Two USB-C ports. | Charge the laptop and the phone from one charger. |
| Timbrado automático de recibos. | La nómina sale con los recibos ya timbrados: no timbra uno por uno. |

Use the benefit only if the brief supports it. Charging two devices from one
charger is what two ports means, so it is safe. A benefit that needs a fact the brief does
not give (a weight, a material, a speed) is an invented claim.

## Objections and FAQ

List the visitor's objections before writing sections. The usual ones:

- Price: how much, what is included, what happens when the trial ends.
- Risk: can I cancel, is there a refund, is my data or money safe.
- Effort: what do I have to do, how long does setup take.
- Fit: will it work for my case, my city, my tools.
- Next step: what happens after I click or submit, and how fast.
- Trust: who is behind this, how long have they done it.

Answer each one beside the action it blocks. The FAQ is the backstop for the
rest. NN/G found visible prices, free trials without a card, case studies and
demos to be the most reliable ways to remove B2B doubt.

FAQ rules:

- Questions in the reader's words, the way they would ask it: "Can I cancel
  any time?", "¿Puedo cancelar cuando quiera?".
- Answer first, in one to three sentences, then link to detail if there is
  any.
- Order by how much the question blocks the decision.
- A question belongs when the visitor would ask it and the brief can answer
  it, or when leaving it unanswered would block the decision. In the second
  case one placeholder answer is right.
- Do not add a question the brief does not raise only to place a placeholder
  under it. If most answers in the FAQ would be placeholders, shorten it to
  the answerable ones and move the gaps to the `Placeholders` list.
- Drop the FAQ if you would have to invent the questions.

## Call-to-action hierarchy and labels

- One primary action per page, with the same label at every main action
  (hero, form, final section), because a reader who sees two labels wonders
  whether they are two actions. The header may use a shorter form of the
  same label (see below). Unbounce's guidance is to designate one main call to action and
  let the repeats catch readers lower down.
- One secondary action, lower commitment than the primary (see a demo,
  read the pricing, call us). Stripe pairs "Get started" with "Sign up with
  Google"; Notion pairs "Get Notion free" with "Request a demo".
- The label is a verb plus what the visitor gets: "Book a 20-minute demo",
  "Reservar cupo". A label like "Submit" or "Learn more" names no
  outcome.
- Under the primary button, one line that says first what the click does
  (creates an account, opens checkout, sends the request). Cost, what you
  need or when someone replies may follow as an addition, only from the
  brief.
- When the button leads to a form on the same page, describe what the
  visitor does next ("Choose a class time in the form below"), not the button sending
  something.
- A long action label repeated many times reads heavy. Use the full label at
  the main actions (hero, form, final section); a shorter form of it may sit
  in the header.
- A first-person label ("Start my free trial") won one test by 90% in click
  rate; the same author reportedly saw a loss on a payment page and no effect
  in Danish. Treat it as a hypothesis to test, and do not hard-code it. No
  Spanish test exists.
- A welded arrow at the end of a button label adds nothing; let the words say
  what happens.

## Form copy

The form is copy too, and unguided agents skip it. Write all of this:

- A visible label for every field, because a placeholder is not a label.
  What the field is for goes in the label. Hint text, if any, is a format
  example only: a phone pattern, a postcode pattern. Never a person's name,
  and never a real place, which reads as a service area the brief may not
  give. Stock text such as "you@example.com" tells the reader nobody wrote
  the form.
- Hint and error text agree with each other. An error that says "numbers
  only" under a hint showing a plus sign and spaces contradicts itself;
  pick the format the form accepts and show that one.
- One line above or below the form saying what the data is for and what
  happens next: who contacts the visitor, by which channel, within what time.
  Write it only from what the brief states. When the brief is silent, the
  line is a placeholder alone, with no sentence in front of it: a stated data
  use, a reply channel or a response time you made up is a promise the
  business did not make.
- The submit label: the same as the primary action, or its natural last
  step ("Send request" after "Request a quote").
- The confirmation message: what was received, and what happens next and
  when only as far as the brief says; the rest is a placeholder.
- Error messages for each required field, in the page's language, saying how
  to fix it ("Escribe tu teléfono, solo números").
- For a waitlist: what joining gives the visitor and how often you will
  write. Only state a frequency or a privacy promise the user has confirmed.

Ask for as few fields as the action needs: email only for a waitlist, the
fields the brief names for a lead form.

## Writing in Spanish

Evidence here is thin: vendor guidance on localisation and one observed
Chilean page. The points marked as judgement are this skill's advice, with
the reason given.

- Choose the pronoun per country and audience before you write a word, and
  keep it on every line, buttons and error messages included. Spain: tú is
  common, banks and SaaS included; vosotros for the informal plural. Mexico
  and most of Latin America: ustedes for the plural, no vosotros. Colombia:
  usted is the norm in commercial and B2B copy. Argentina: voseo ("vos
  tenés") at every level. Chile: Fintual, a regulated investment company,
  uses tú. Judgement: tú suits Chilean consumer pages;
  usted suits formal B2B or an older audience that expects it. Written
  Chilean voseo ("tú podís") reads as spoken slang on a business page.
- Use the target country's words: ordenador or computadora, móvil or
  celular, coche or carro or auto, presupuesto or cotización. Pick one
  country per page. A forced "neutral" mix sounds like nobody.
- Write the Spanish fresh from the brief. A line translated from English keeps
  the English rhythm and the English stock phrases, which is how most of the
  Spanish tells in `tells.md` arise.
- Spanish runs longer than English. One vendor puts it at 15 to 30% (a
  figure that could not be verified). Write headlines short in Spanish
  first, and tell the designer which labels are long.
- H1 and section headings (judgement): about eight words at most, one
  sentence, and no colon list inside a heading ("Clases, horarios y
  precios: todo en un lugar"). When the limit forces a cut, cut the
  category words first and keep the brief's most specific fact in the
  heading. The displaced words go to the subhead or the next line, not to a
  separate generic section. A heading
  that narrates a whole process ("Qué pasa desde que te inscribes hasta que
  recibes tu primer pedido") reads as a sentence from a manual; keep the job
  and cut the route: "Tu primer pedido".
- Write each job as a natural sentence, not as a label with a colon. A row
  like "Entrega: la hacemos nosotros." reads as a form someone filled in.
  Write "Te llevamos la caja a la puerta los jueves" if the brief says so.
- The narrator rule below applies to headings too: a "nosotros" page does
  not title a section with the business in the third person. "Clases de la
  escuela" on a "nosotros" page becomes "Nuestras clases"; "Equipo de la
  escuela" becomes "Quiénes te enseñan".
- Headings use sentence case: only the first word and proper nouns take a
  capital ("Cómo funciona la prueba", not "Cómo Funciona la Prueba").
- Open questions and exclamations with ¿ and ¡.
- Keep one narrator. Write as "nosotros" or name the business in the third
  person, and do not switch between them. A brief written in the third person
  ("el estudio se encarga de...") is not a model for the page voice. The same
  applies in English.
- Numbers and money (judgement, not from the research; check with the user
  when unsure). Chile and Argentina: a point for thousands and a comma for
  decimals (1.200.000; 2,5), and $ marks pesos. Mexico: a comma for thousands
  and a point for decimals (1,200,000; 2.5), and $ marks pesos. Spain: a
  comma for decimals; for thousands the point is common and the Real
  Academia recommends a space; euros are written after the number (75 €).
  In the three peso countries, write "USD" for a dollar price so it is not
  read as pesos. For any other country, ask the user which format the
  business uses.
- The English findings above (reading level, first-person buttons,
  scannability) were not tested in Spanish. Use them as hypotheses.

## Worked examples in English

Each example states its brief first. The briefs are invented for teaching;
the proof slots stay as placeholders because no proof was given.

**Brief (invented):** scheduling tool for restaurant managers. Builds the weekly rota
from staff availability; staff swap shifts in the app with manager approval.
30-day free trial. Proof: none.

- Before: "Elevate your team's scheduling experience." Inflated verb, no
  category, no audience, could be any product.
- After, headline: "Weekly rotas for restaurants, built from your staff's
  availability."
- After, subhead: "Staff mark when they can work, you get a draft rota, and
  shift swaps come to you for approval. Free for 30 days."
- After, primary action: "Start the 30-day trial"; under it, the line the
  brief supports about what the trial requires, or a placeholder.
- After, in action form instead: "Build next week's rota from the hours your
  staff can work." The same facts, led by what the manager gets done; pick
  this form when the brief's strongest fact is the action, not the category.

**Brief (invented):** cast-iron pan, 26 cm, pre-seasoned, 2.4 kg, lifetime warranty,
75 USD. Proof: none.

- Before: "Cook like never before." Promises nothing checkable.
- After, headline: "A 26 cm cast-iron pan, seasoned before it ships."
- After, subhead: "2.4 kg, 75 USD, and a warranty for life."
- Before, feature section heading: "Built to last." After: "Covered for life,
  and heavy enough to keep the heat." The second half relies on the weight in
  the brief; drop it if the weight is not there.

**Brief (invented):** a new brand's first two-person tent, sold ahead of
production, no proof.

- Before: the product name alone as the headline. Nobody knows the name yet.
- After: the name as a small label, and a headline that states the category
  and the one fact from the brief that sets it apart.

## Worked examples in Spanish

**Brief (inventado):** clases de natación para adultos que no saben nadar,
en una piscina temperada de Concepción. Grupos de seis. Cuatro clases por
$48.000. Español de Chile, tú. Pruebas: ninguna.

- Antes: "Lleva tu relación con el agua al siguiente nivel." Frase hecha,
  sin clase, sin ciudad, sin precio.
- Después, título: "Aprende a nadar de adulto, en grupos de seis, en
  Concepción."
- Después, bajada: "Piscina temperada. Cuatro clases por $48.000."
- Después, botón: "Reservar cupo". Bajo el botón, lo que hace el clic según
  el brief, o `[PLACEHOLDER: qué pasa al reservar: pago, confirmación, fecha de inicio]`.

**Brief (inventado):** software de nómina para pymes en México. Calcula la nómina,
timbra los recibos y genera el archivo para el banco. Prueba de 15 días.
Español de México, usted, porque el comprador es el contador o el dueño.
Pruebas: ninguna.

- Antes: "Potencia tu negocio con soluciones innovadoras." Verbo inflado,
  adjetivo vacío, y tutea a un comprador que espera usted.
- Después, título: "Calcule y timbre la nómina de su pyme en una sola
  pantalla."
- Después, bajada: "Los recibos quedan timbrados y el archivo para el banco
  listo para subir. Pruebe 15 días."
- Después, pregunta frecuente: "¿Qué pasa al terminar la prueba?" con la
  respuesta del brief, o un placeholder si el brief no la da.

**Brief (inventado):** cajas de verdura de temporada de tres huertos de
Cholula, entrega semanal en Puebla. Caja chica 280 MXN, caja grande 450
MXN. Suscripción que se pausa cuando quieras. Español de México, tú.
Pruebas: ninguna.

Errores típicos de un agente sin guía, escritos aquí para este brief:

- Título: "Come rico, vive mejor." Par de fragmentos sin producto, sin
  ciudad, sin precio, y los tres están en el brief.
- Bajada: "Nos preocupamos por cada detalle para que tú solo disfrutes."
  Promesa vaga que sirve a cualquier negocio de comida.
- Intro de sección: "¿Quieres comer más sano pero no sabes cómo? Aquí te lo
  contamos." Pregunta gancho que repite el título de la sección.

The fix in each case is the same: replace the soft line with the facts the
brief gives (what is in the box, from where, which city, what it costs, how
to pause) and put an unknown fact in a placeholder.
