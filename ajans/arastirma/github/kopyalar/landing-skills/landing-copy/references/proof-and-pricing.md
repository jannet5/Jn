## Why proof is never invented

Proof that is not real hurts the user twice.

Legal risk. In the US, the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials
(16 CFR Part 465, announced August 2024) bans fake reviews and testimonials,
AI-generated ones included, and bans publishing testimonials the business
knew or should have known were fake. It also covers undisclosed reviews by
insiders, suppressing negative reviews, and buying fake followers or
engagement, and it allows civil penalties. In the EU, a secondary source
reports that the 2019 Omnibus Directive added two practices to the list that
is always unfair under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive: claiming
that reviews come from real users without reasonable steps to check, and
submitting or commissioning false reviews or endorsements. That report says
it applies from 28 May 2022; the official text was not read for this skill,
so treat the EU detail as reported, not confirmed. Other countries were not
researched. None of this is legal advice; it is the reason the rule exists.

Trust risk. Readers check. A testimonial from a name with no company, a logo
of a firm that never bought, a round number that nobody can reproduce: each
one, once doubted, makes every other claim on the page doubtful. Baymard even
found that an invented trust seal raised perceived security, which is exactly
why inventing one is deception and not decoration.

So: write only proof the brief gives. Everything else is a visible
placeholder that the user fills or deletes before launch.

## What counts as proof, and how to present each kind

| Kind | Counts when | Present it as |
|---|---|---|
| Testimonial | A real person said it and agreed to its use. | Full name, job title, company; short and specific, ideally with an outcome. NN/G found B2B readers check the title and company, and respond to quotes that show doubt turning into confidence. |
| Customer logos | Real customers who gave permission. | Recognisable logos, with a number or milestone beside them. A logo strip alone is weak proof, and the logo wall is a stock pattern of generated pages. |
| Case study | A real project with a real client. | Problem, what was done, result with a number, client named if permitted. |
| Numbers | The business can reproduce the figure. | The figure with its unit and context: "[number] locks fitted in [area] since [year]" says more than a bare "[number]+". Give the baseline for any improvement claim. |
| Ratings and reviews | Collected from real buyers, unfiltered. | Average, count and source. Suppressing negative reviews is itself banned in the US. |
| Track record | Years in business, completed jobs, units sold. | As a sentence with context, near the action it supports. |
| Milestones | Real and dated: launch, release, funding, a sold-out edition. | With the date. A dated element shows the business is active. |
| Method | The business can explain how it works or checks quality. | A section that explains it. Oura's Spanish page has a section on how they measure sleep and validate accuracy. |
| Trust seals | Real certifications or payment-provider marks. | One or two, next to the payment or form step. |

Every number in the copy needs a source in the brief. A round figure with no
source (a "10,000+", a "99.9%", a "24/7") reads as filler because generated
pages produce exactly that row.

## Giving real proof its moment

When the brief has proof, unguided agents tend to bury it: a bare number row
with no context, or the one strong fact at the end of a paragraph. Do this
instead:

- Put the strongest proof where the doubt arises: for a service, near the
  top; for a waitlist, beside the field.
- Say it once. The same figure in the hero, a proof band and the footer
  stops reading as evidence and starts reading as padding.
- Use the brief's own terms. If the brief says (an invented example) "11
  years in business", write that, not "11 years fitting high-security locks": the
  second adds a claim about what the years were spent on.
- Context for a figure (what kind of work, where, since when) comes only
  from the brief. If it is not there, ask, or add a placeholder for it; do
  not supply a plausible city or category.

## When there is no proof

Say so honestly, and use what is verifiable instead. NN/G recommends free
trials without a card and real demos for B2B; the rest of this list is
practitioner advice.

- Who is behind it: the founder or maker, by name, with relevant background
  the user confirms.
- How it works, shown concretely: the real interface, the process step by
  step.
- Risk removed: a free trial, a refund, a guarantee, but only the terms the
  business offers.
- An honest launch line with an absolute date: launched in [month and year],
  first edition, first product.
  New is a fact; it explains the missing reviews better than silence does.
- How payment and data are handled, when the brief says (which payment
  processor, where data is stored).
- Building the proof: pilot clients, named beta users with permission. That is
  advice for the user, not copy for the page.

What not to write: logos, quotes, counters, star ratings, "as seen in" press
mentions, "Trusted by" lines, or user counts the brief does not give. Drop a
testimonials section rather than fill it.

## Placeholders

A placeholder is visible and unmistakable, so nobody ships it by accident and
no reader takes it for real:

```text
[PLACEHOLDER: one testimonial from a client, with full name, job title and company, and written permission to publish]
```

Rules:

- The form is always `[PLACEHOLDER: what is needed]`, with enough detail that
  the user knows what to supply.
- Never write a placeholder that looks real: no sample name, no sample
  company, no sample quote, no stock person. A realistic placeholder gets
  shipped.
- Every placeholder in the copy appears again in the `Placeholders` section
  at the end of `landing/copy.md`, with where it sits and whether the page
  can launch without it.
- Placeholders also cover facts that are not proof: a response time, a
  contact phone, an absolute date, a refund policy.

## Pricing

Show the price when the business can. NN/G found that hidden prices make a
company look dubious and send the buyer elsewhere to research; a price shown
early sets expectations.

- The plan structure follows the real offer. One plan: show one price, with
  what it includes. Tiers only when the business has tiers. A "recommended"
  marker only when the business recommends a plan.
- Three tiers with a ringed "popular" middle plan is a stock pattern of
  generated pages, and none of 30 well-made home pages studied showed a
  three-tier table; pricing was a link or a usage-based section. Three plans
  is common on pricing pages, but nothing tests it as better.
- When plans differ, show only the attributes that differ, in the same order
  for each plan, so the rows compare. NN/G found unclear differences lead
  buyers to wrong choices.
- Bundles and longer terms: state the saving in money, and show the
  arithmetic if you derived it. With single classes at 15 EUR and a 10-class
  pass at 120 EUR, the pass saves 30 EUR (10 × 15 = 150; 150 − 120 = 30).
  Say "save 30 EUR", and check the sum. The same applies to a yearly price
  against a monthly one.
- Write only the inclusions and limits the brief states. "Unlimited",
  "all features", "priority support" are claims; if the brief does not say
  them, they are invented.
- Next to the price, answer the price objections: what happens when the
  trial ends, whether a card is needed, how to cancel. Use placeholders
  where the brief is silent.
- The decoy effect (adding a worse option to steer the choice) comes from lab
  experiments with students. It is not a reason to add a plan the business
  does not sell.

## Pricing when prices are not published

Some services quote per job. A two-line "it depends" in its own section is
not an answer. Write:

- Why there is no fixed price, only if the brief gives the reason or the
  cost drivers. If it does not, write no reason: state what the brief states
  (prices are not published, and how a price is given) and put a
  placeholder for the cost drivers. A reason you supply, however obvious,
  is a claim the business did not make.
- What the visitor gets instead and when, as the brief describes it (a
  quote once the job is scoped, a price for a sample case), with its
  turnaround if the brief gives it, or a placeholder.
- Whether the first step costs anything, only if the brief says.
- If the business will share typical ranges or a "from" price, ask for them;
  NN/G recommends typical examples when an exact price is impossible.

Put this answer next to the form, because cost is the question the visitor
has at the moment of deciding to get in touch.
