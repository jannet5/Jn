## How to read these sequences

No study tests one section order against another for any landing type. The
sequences below are convention: what well-made pages visibly do, combined with
practitioner guidance. Use them as a list of jobs to do in a sensible order,
and change the order when this visitor's decision needs it.

Each sequence orders jobs, not layouts. "Three benefits" means three things
the product does for the reader, which the art direction may show as one long
section, a list, or three different blocks. It does not mean three equal cards.
The default skeleton (hero, logo bar, three features, testimonials, pricing,
FAQ, final CTA, footer) applied to every product is itself a recognised sign of
generated pages, because it comes from a template and not from the questions a
buyer asks.

## Rules that apply to every type

- Every section earns its place. Before you keep a section, name the question
  it answers for the visitor. If you cannot, drop it.
- Drop a section when its content would have to be invented: a logo strip
  with no real logos, a speakers section before speakers are announced, an
  FAQ whose questions nobody has asked, a testimonials block with no
  testimonials.
- Put the final call to action after the FAQ, so a reader who scrolled to the
  bottom with a last doubt sees it answered and then sees the action.
- Answer an objection next to the action it blocks. A "cancel any time" line
  belongs under the subscribe button, not in the FAQ only.
- Close by returning to the opening claim in one sentence. Several well-made
  SaaS pages do this. Write your own sentence: two well-known pages already
  share the same closing line, which shows how fast a good device becomes a
  stock one.
- If the product has a real dated element (a release, a changelog entry, an
  event date, a season opening), it is good evidence that people are working on
  it. Ask for it. Never invent one.
- The footer carries the ways to reach a person: email, phone, address,
  messaging channel, hours, whichever the business uses. If the brief gives
  none, add a placeholder. A service page whose only contact is a form loses
  the visitors who would rather call.

## SaaS

| Order | Section | Job | Drop when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | Say what it is, for whom, and what to do next. Headline, subhead, primary action, product visual. | Never. |
| 2 | Proof near the top | Cheap credibility before the reader scrolls: real logos with a number or milestone beside them. | No real, permitted logos and no number. Do not fill the slot. |
| 3 | What it does, one section per job | Answer "how does it work" with the jobs the reader has today (plan the rota, cover a sick day). As many sections as the product has jobs. | Never as a whole; each job section goes if the brief does not support it. |
| 4 | Proof | Reduce risk: named testimonial, case, real numbers, changelog. | No proof. Put the no-proof substitutes from `proof-and-pricing.md` here instead, or nothing. |
| 5 | Pricing | Remove the "how much" objection. | Sales-led with no public price; even then give a range or "from" if the business can. |
| 6 | FAQ | Clear the remaining objections. | You would have to invent the questions. |
| 7 | Final action | Catch readers who scanned to the bottom. | Never. |

Observed on well-made pages: sections named after a stage of work or a job,
a product view drawn from the real interface, a changelog on the home page,
and pricing as a link or a usage-based section rather than a tier table.

## Physical product

| Order | Section | Job | Drop when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | Product image in use, one-line benefit, price or "from", buy button. | Never. |
| 2 | Uses | Three to five benefits, each tied to a moment of use. | Never; cut the count to what the brief supports. |
| 3 | Details | Specs, materials, what is in the box. Only facts from the brief. | The product is simple enough that the uses say it all. |
| 4 | Reviews | Real ratings with count and source. | No reviews collected. |
| 5 | Why this one | Comparison with the alternative the buyer considers. | One product with no real comparison to make. |
| 6 | Shipping, returns, warranty | Remove purchase risk. Put a one-line version next to the buy button too. | Never for a store; for a pre-order, state what applies. |
| 7 | FAQ | Remaining questions. | Questions would be invented. |
| 8 | Final buy action | Catch the scanner. | Never. |

For a spec-led product whose buyers check every detail before buying
(cameras, bikes, audio gear), ask the user what such a buyer needs to see
(the full specification, options, compatibility, who makes it) and write a placeholder
for each item the brief does not give. A comparer who cannot find a detail
assumes the worst or buys elsewhere (judgement).

Keep delivery, returns and guarantee near the buy button, not only in the
footer. Baymard's checkout research found that 19% of surveyed shoppers had
abandoned a checkout because they did not trust the site with card details;
trust has to be visible where the money is asked for.

## Service or studio

| Order | Section | Job | Drop when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | Who you help, the outcome, the next step ("book a call", "request a quote"). | Never. |
| 2 | Proof, early | Named clients, results, years, completed projects, with context. | No proof at all; then lead with the process. |
| 3 | Services | Two to four, each outcome-first, with the concrete scope from the brief. | Never; list only services the brief names. |
| 4 | Process | Three to five steps that show what happens after first contact. | The service is a single deliverable. |
| 5 | Selected work | Case studies or projects. | No photos or cases yet; leave a placeholder and say when they arrive. |
| 6 | About | The people. | Keep it when the person is the brand. |
| 7 | FAQ | Price range, timeline, what the client must provide. | Questions would be invented. |
| 8 | Contact form | The conversion action, with what happens next. | Never. |

NN/G's B2B research found buyers read the job title and company of a
testimonial's author, want case studies, and distrust hidden prices. When
prices are not published, say so and say what the visitor gets instead, as
the brief describes it (a quote once the job is scoped, typical ranges if
the business will share them). Give a reason or cost drivers only if the brief gives them; see
`proof-and-pricing.md`. A two-line "it depends" in its own section is a
non-answer. A service section that lists the brief's services in bare
sentences is a stub; see "Thin sections" below.

A studio whose work speaks for it can lead with the work grouped by
discipline and sell very little; that suits a studio with a portfolio, not
one without photographs.

## Waitlist

| Order | Section | Job | Drop when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | One promise, one field, one button. | Never. |
| 2 | What it does | Three short points on what the product is and does. | Never. |
| 3 | Why it is different | The specific reason to wait for this one. | Nothing true to say yet. |
| 4 | Early-access terms | Position, price, date, what joining commits the visitor to. | No terms beyond "we will email you". |
| 5 | One proof | Founder credibility, press, a real signup count. | None; leave it out. |
| 6 | FAQ | When, how much, what happens to my email. | Questions would be invented. |
| 7 | Repeat form | Catch the scanner. | Never. |

Ask for the email only. If the product does not exist yet, end the page after
the second form. Never show a signup counter that is not real. Say what the
visitor receives after joining and how often, but only what the business has
committed to; a line such as "we write only on launch day" is a commitment
the user has to make, so ask or use a placeholder.

## Event

| Order | Section | Job | Drop when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hero | Name, date, place or "online", price or "free", register. | Never. Date and place must be here. |
| 2 | Who it is for | Let the reader self-select. | Never. |
| 3 | Agenda and speakers | What happens, who speaks. | Not announced: write "Speakers announced on [date]" or drop. |
| 4 | Venue and logistics | Getting there, timing, access. | Online only; then say how to join. |
| 5 | Tickets | Prices, what each includes, deadline. | Free with no tiers; fold into the hero. |
| 6 | Past editions, sponsors | Proof. | First edition, no sponsors. |
| 7 | FAQ | Refunds, recordings, accessibility. | Questions would be invented. |
| 8 | Final register action | Catch the scanner. | Never. |

When registration closes, say so on the page instead of leaving a dead button.

## Mixed types

Briefs often combine types: a product sold through a waitlist or ahead of
production, a studio that also runs a yearly event. Take the hero and the
conversion action from the type that matches the action, and the middle
sections from the type that matches what is sold (product details, specs,
delivery). Selling ahead of production adds questions a store page does not
have: when it ships, what the visitor pays now, whether that is refundable,
who is making it. Answer each from the
brief or with a placeholder.

## Thin sections

Briefs are often short, and the visitor still needs some sections the brief
says little about: what the service includes, how the process starts, what
it costs. Two ways to get these wrong: invent the detail, or restate the
brief in three bare sentences and move on. A stub section tells the visitor
nothing they did not get from the hero.

Instead:

- Write the first concrete step the brief does support. If the brief names a
  first lesson, a trial or a sign-up, that step is the content: what the
  visitor does, what they give, what they get back, as far as the brief
  says.
- A list of services or steps in the form "X: we do it" is a stub. Give each
  one natural sentence written from what the brief gives, and a placeholder
  for the detail it does not give.
- Say what happens in that step next to the call to action, in one line.
- Put a placeholder for each detail the visitor needs and the brief lacks
  (what the step involves, how long it takes, what it costs), and list it.
- If even the first step is unknown, merge the section into its neighbour
  rather than leave a stub, and ask the user.

## Naming sections

Name each section after the job it does or give it a sentence with a point of
view. Generic labels fit any product and tell the reader nothing.

| Generic | Named after the job |
|---|---|
| Features | Shift swaps your staff can approve from their phone |
| How it works | What happens in the week after you send the form |
| Why choose us | What the monthly fee covers that a one-off repair does not |
| Testimonials | (drop the section if there are none) |
| Nuestros servicios | Clases de natación para adultos en grupos de seis |
| Our story | Two founders, one workshop in [town] (from the brief) |

The right-hand examples are illustrations; take the actual words from the
brief.

## Length and reading level

People read little on the web: NN/G's studies (1997 to 2006) found visitors
read at most about 28% of the words on an average page, and far fewer readers
reach the fourth paragraph than the first. NN/G's web-writing study found
concise, scannable and objective text together made the same site 124% more
usable than promotional text; the study is old, the direction has held.
Unbounce's benchmark found English pages written at a 5th to 7th grade reading
level converted better, and longer pages and harder words correlated with
lower conversion. That is a correlation across about 41,000 English pages, not
a controlled test, and nothing comparable exists for Spanish.

What to do with that: short sentences, common words, the reader's own words
for the problem, one idea per paragraph, the most important sentence first in
each section.
